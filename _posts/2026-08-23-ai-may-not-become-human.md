_The greatest risk of artificial intelligence may not be that machines become indistinguishable from us, but that we rebuild society around the things machines can understand._

Walk into an older bank and notice everything that is not strictly necessary.

There may be chairs arranged around a table, plants in the corners, framed paintings on the walls, newspapers near the entrance, and a person behind a desk who looks up when you enter. The building is warm in winter. Someone can tell that you are confused before you have found the words to explain why. None of this is required to perform the bank's most basic transaction: you provide identification and instructions, and the bank moves or stores your money.

A hospital room contains similar excesses. A window, a plant, a painting, a television, a chair for a visitor—none of these performs surgery, administers medication, or measures blood pressure. A reception area may contain magazines that nobody finishes reading and artwork that nobody came specifically to see.

These details are easy to dismiss as decoration. But they are also evidence that an institution remembers it is serving a human being.

As artificial intelligence becomes embedded in more of everyday life, I suspect that the most important change will not be that machines gradually become human. It will be that our institutions, buildings, and expectations are gradually redesigned around machines. The world will become easier for automated systems to navigate—and less accommodating to the parts of us that cannot be converted into a clean input, measurable output, or optimized transaction.

The danger is not necessarily artificial intelligence itself. It is the temptation to rebuild human environments according to machine logic.

## The value of what appears unnecessary

Efficiency asks a reasonable question: What is required to complete the task?

Humanity asks a different one: What makes the experience bearable, dignified, or meaningful?

The difference can look wasteful. Art does not process a bank deposit. A receptionist does not increase the computational accuracy of a medical diagnosis. A sheltered vestibule around an ATM is more expensive than mounting the machine on an exterior wall. A waiting room takes up space that could be monetized or eliminated.

Yet these apparently unnecessary features can produce real effects.

In an influential [1984 study published in _Science_](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6143402/), patients recovering from surgery were compared based on whether their hospital windows faced a natural scene or a brick wall. Those with the natural view experienced shorter postoperative stays, received fewer negative evaluations from nurses, and used fewer strong pain medications. Later research on art and healing environments has been more mixed, and [systematic reviews appropriately caution that the evidence remains limited and heterogeneous](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26583656/). Nevertheless, the broader lesson is important: environmental qualities that appear secondary to the official transaction can still affect the person experiencing it.

A hospital is not merely a facility in which medical procedures occur. It is a place where frightened, tired, sick, and grieving people spend time. A bank is not merely a database containing account balances. It is where people ask for help with decisions that may determine whether they can buy a home, resolve fraud, or recover from a financial mistake.

Institutions serve people in states that cannot always be anticipated by a decision tree.

The newspaper in the waiting room, the chair beside the hospital bed, and the employee willing to listen are small acknowledgments of this fact. They tell us that waiting is an experience, illness is frightening, confusion is normal, and a person may need more than the completion of a transaction.

## AI does not have to become human to reorganize human life

Public discussion often treats intelligence as if it were a single ladder. Humans stand on one rung, current AI stands somewhere below us, and artificial general intelligence will eventually climb past us.

Reality is less orderly.

AI systems can already exceed most people at particular forms of calculation, pattern recognition, retrieval, coding, and structured problem-solving. At the same time, they can fail unexpectedly when a problem changes form, requires sustained contextual judgment, or falls outside the patterns represented in their training and tools.

It would therefore be inaccurate to say that AI is simply approaching a fixed plateau. The [2026 Stanford AI Index](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report) reports rapid gains across coding, scientific reasoning, mathematics, and multimodal tasks. But high benchmark performance is not the same as possessing human understanding. Earlier evaluations of longer-duration work found that, while AI agents could outperform experts on short tasks, [human performance again became stronger as tasks extended over longer time horizons](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2025-ai-index-report/technical-performance).

The crucial distinction is not whether AI is "smart." It is what kind of intelligence an institution rewards.

AI is particularly valuable when a problem has recognizable inputs, observable outputs, repeatable patterns, and a metric by which success can be judged. This includes an enormous range of useful work. But a human encounter contains information that is difficult to formalize: hesitation, embarrassment, trust, grief, social context, moral responsibility, and the possibility that the stated problem is not the real problem.

Modern AI does not literally solve every mathematical or scientific problem by following a written recipe. It learns statistical patterns, generates candidate responses, invokes tools, and increasingly performs forms of search and reasoning. Nevertheless, its success still depends on making the world legible to computation.

This creates an institutional incentive: when the machine cannot reliably accommodate the full complexity of the environment, simplify the environment.

The customer is given a menu. The unusual question is redirected into a standard category. The employee is replaced by a kiosk. The room becomes an interface. The conversation becomes a form.

Eventually, the physical institution may disappear altogether.

## From a room to a wall

Banks provide a useful example because this transition has already begun, although it would be misleading to attribute it entirely to AI. Mobile banking, consolidation, changing consumer preferences, real-estate costs, and the pandemic have all contributed to the decline of physical branches.

From 2019 to 2023, the total number of U.S. bank branches fell by 5.6 percent. Over the same period, the number of banking deserts increased by 217, and approximately 760,000 additional people came to live in one, according to the [Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia](https://www.philadelphiafed.org/community-development/credit-and-capital/u-s-bank-branch-closures-and-banking-deserts).

For many customers, digital banking is genuinely better. Depositing a check from a phone is faster than driving to a branch. An ATM is more convenient than waiting for a teller. Automation can expand access, reduce costs, and eliminate pointless errands.

But substitution is not always equivalence.

In communities affected by branch closures, Federal Reserve listening sessions found that people could replace many—but not all—branch services through local or technological alternatives. Some customers reported [greater costs and reduced convenience](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/bank-branch-access-in-rural-communities.pdf), with the burden falling especially heavily on people with limited transportation, internet access, or comfort with digital systems.

The change is therefore larger than moving the same service online. A branch is a place. An application is an interface. An ATM is a wall.

When an institution becomes a wall, the customer must arrive already knowing what the institution expects. The interface cannot easily notice that an elderly customer is becoming overwhelmed, that a person does not understand a fee, or that someone describing one problem is actually experiencing another.

This limitation is visible in automated customer service. The U.S. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau has documented cases in which financial chatbots trapped customers in repetitive "doom loops," particularly when their problems fell outside the systems' predefined capabilities. The agency warned that [automated systems without a reliable path to a human representative can produce diminished service and tangible harm](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/data-research/research-reports/chatbots-in-consumer-finance/).

The chatbot did not become frustrated with the customer. It did not behave maliciously. It simply continued operating within the structure it had been given.

That is precisely the concern.

## Humanity lives in the unmeasured margin

An optimization system can preserve only what its objective recognizes.

If the goal of a bank is defined as completing the largest number of transactions at the lowest cost, then a conversation that takes twenty minutes appears inefficient. A lobby appears underused. A painting appears economically unproductive. An employee who spends time reassuring a confused customer appears slower than an automated system that closes the interaction.

But perhaps the conversation prevented a catastrophic decision. Perhaps the lobby provided one of the few safe, warm indoor spaces available to an elderly person. Perhaps speaking to another human restored enough trust for a customer to ask the question they were embarrassed to ask a screen.

These outcomes are difficult to place on a quarterly balance sheet. That does not make them unreal.

The [U.S. Surgeon General's advisory on social connection](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK595227/) emphasizes that the design of transportation, housing, public areas, and green spaces directly affects opportunities for human interaction. Social connection is not merely a private emotional preference; it is associated with health, resilience, and community well-being.

A reception desk is not the same thing as a public square, and a bank lobby is not automatically a community center. Still, the gradual removal of staffed, shared spaces matters because social life is partly created through repeated low-stakes encounters. We lose something when every interaction becomes an isolated exchange between one person and one screen.

We also risk excluding anyone whose needs do not fit the standard path: the elderly, disabled people, those with limited literacy or English proficiency, people facing unusual circumstances, or simply anyone who makes a mistake.

Human systems have historically contained redundancy because humans are variable. We ask again. We explain differently. We notice discomfort. We make exceptions. What appears inefficient from the perspective of throughput may be essential from the perspective of dignity.

## AI can also make institutions more human

This outcome is not inevitable.

The same technology used to eliminate human contact can support it. In a study of 5,172 customer-service workers, an AI assistant increased productivity by approximately 15 percent, with the largest gains among less-experienced workers. It also improved customer sentiment and employee retention. In this case, documented in the [study _Generative AI at Work_](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161), AI did not replace the human encounter. It helped a person conduct it more effectively.

That suggests a more useful distinction than "AI versus humanity": substitution versus augmentation.

If AI completes paperwork so that a physician can spend more time with a patient, it may increase the humanity of medicine. If it helps a bank employee quickly understand a complicated account history, it may improve the conversation. If it translates speech, supports disabled users, or handles routine work while leaving humans available for unusual and consequential problems, it can expand access rather than contract it.

The technology does not dictate which path we take.

As the National Institute of Standards and Technology explains in its [AI Risk Management Framework](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.100-1.pdf), AI systems are socio-technical. Their effects emerge not only from the model, but from the people, organizations, incentives, and environments surrounding its use.

The real threat is therefore not that AI lacks humanity. A tool cannot be blamed for lacking the needs of the people it was built to serve.

The threat is that organizations will treat efficiency as the only measurable good and redesign the world accordingly.

## Automate the transaction; preserve the encounter

We need a design principle for the AI age:

**Automate the transaction, but preserve the encounter.**

Let AI perform repetitive processing. Let it search records, identify patterns, prepare forms, translate instructions, and reduce administrative work. But preserve accessible human escalation. Preserve places where people can ask incomplete questions. Preserve shelter, seating, beauty, and the possibility of being noticed.

The savings produced by automation should not automatically be harvested by removing the human layer. Some of those savings should be reinvested in it.

Organizations should measure more than speed and cost. They should ask whether customers can recover from errors, whether vulnerable people can obtain help, whether a human being remains reachable, and whether the environment communicates dignity rather than mere compliance.

Art, plants, newspapers, comfortable chairs, and conversations may look like inefficiencies because they do not directly execute the transaction. But the transaction is not the whole purpose of a human institution.

AI may eventually become far more capable than it is today. It may surpass us across domains that we currently consider uniquely human. Or it may continue to combine extraordinary competence with surprising limitations.

Either way, we should not wait for a final verdict on machine intelligence before deciding what kind of world to build around it.

AI is not the antithesis of humanity. Optimization without a sufficiently human objective is.

The future will not become less human merely because machines fail to become us. It will become less human if we redesign society so that only the needs machines can recognize are treated as real.
